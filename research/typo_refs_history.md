# Typography refs: Europe's historical brakes and breakthroughs (before about 2000)

Research for "I'm Upping My P(doom) — EU Edition", 1 Oct 2026. Short texts, numbers, dates and names to set as big type, each with a source.

**Status key**
- **verified**: the primary text, or a solid secondary source that quotes it, was opened.
- **disputed**: the event is real, but a detail, number or wording is contested.
- **apocryphal**: there is no contemporary evidence. Do not set it as fact. It can still be set, labelled as LEGEND.

URLs marked *(listing only)* were seen in search results but not opened. Every other URL was opened.

## Top 10 for type (ranked)

1. **RED FLAG ACT (UK, 1865).** "shall carry a Red Flag constantly displayed". 60 YARDS ahead. 4 MPH in the country, 2 MPH in town. Repealed: 14 MPH from 14 Nov 1896, and a red flag was torn in two at the breakfast before the Emancipation Run. [#1]
2. **"JEDE STRASSENBELEUCHTUNG IST VERWERFLICH"** ("All street lighting is reprehensible"). *Kölnische Zeitung*, 28 Mar 1819. It gives seven reasons, including "sie macht Pferde scheu und Diebe kühn" ("it makes horses shy and thieves bold"). [#2]
3. **PRINTING ABOLISHED "SOUS PEINE DE LA HART"** ("on pain of the noose"). Francis I, 13 Jan 1535. Lifted 44 days later. [#3]
4. **COFFEE HOUSES "PUT DOWN AND SUPPRESSED"** as "the great resort of Idle and disaffected persons". Charles II, 29 Dec 1675. He backed down 10 days later. [#4]
5. **"VAGUE, BUT EXCITING"**. Mike Sendall's note on Tim Berners-Lee's proposal, CERN, March 1989. The Web was released royalty-free on 30 Apr 1993. [#30]
6. **"JUST A HOBBY, WON'T BE BIG AND PROFESSIONAL LIKE GNU"**. Linus Torvalds, Helsinki, 25 Aug 1991. [#31]
7. **"A SUPERABUNDANCE OF MESSENGERS, ERRAND BOYS"**. William Preece of the Post Office, explaining why Britain needed the telephone less than America. House of Commons select committee, 2 May 1879. [#14]
8. **"AN ACT FOR THE PUTTING DOWN OF GIGG-MILLS"**. England, 1551. Repealed in 1809, after 257 years. [#10]
9. **SWEDEN BANNED COFFEE FIVE TIMES**, between 1756 and 1823. The last ban ended in 1823. [#6]
10. **"MERRY CHRISTMAS"**. The first SMS, 3 Dec 1992. It ran on GSM, the standard Europe signed in Copenhagen on 7 Sep 1987. The first call was made in Helsinki on 1 Jul 1991. [#28, #29]

**Runners-up**
- France's telegraph law of 2 May 1837: "Quiconque transmettra, sans autorisation, des signaux…" ("Whoever transmits signals without authorisation…"). [#16]
- The ribbon loom, "forbidden throughout all Germany" on 19 Feb 1685 and "burnt in public" in Hamburg. [#7]

- Bertha Benz drove 106 km "without permission of the authorities". [#26]

**Do not set as fact**
- Elizabeth I's "Thou aimest high, Master Lee".
- Wilhelm II's "Ich glaube an das Pferd" ("I believe in the horse").
- The Bavarian doctors' "delirium furiosum".
- The Danzig inventor who was strangled.

## Part 1. The brakes (and how each one ended)

### 1. UK Locomotive Act 1865 ("Red Flag Act") and the Emancipation Run, 1896
- **Dates:** Locomotives Act 1865; amended in 1878. The Locomotives on Highways Act 1896 received royal assent on 14 Aug 1896 and came into force on 14 Nov 1896.
- **Type (s. 3):** "one of such Persons, while any Locomotive is in Motion, shall precede such Locomotive on Foot by not less than Sixty Yards, and shall carry a Red Flag constantly displayed".
- **Numbers:**
  - 1865: a crew of 3; 60 yards; 4 mph in the country, 2 mph in town.
  - 1878: the flag was dropped and the walker moved to 20 yards ahead, with the same speed limits.
  - 1896: vehicles under 3 tons unladen became "light locomotives" with a 14 mph limit. Councils could cut it to 12.
  - It took 31 years to get from the 1865 rules to 1896.
- **How it ended:** The Emancipation Run went from London to Brighton on Saturday 14 Nov 1896, a wet day. It began with a breakfast at the Charing Cross Hotel, where Lord Winchilsea gave "the symbolic tearing in two" of a red flag. The flag was torn, not burned. About 30–33 cars started and 13–17 reached Brighton; sources differ. The run is still held every November.
- **Bonus line:** J. H. Knight was fined 2s 6d in 1895 for driving "without a licence and without a red flag!" (Knight, 1906).
- **Status:** verified. One nuance: the flag itself had been legally dead since 1878. The 1896 organisers played it up as a symbol.
- **Sources:**
  - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Locomotive_Acts
  - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Locomotives_on_Highways_Act_1896
  - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/London_to_Brighton_Veteran_Car_Run (the flag tearing is cited to *Autocar*, 4 Nov 1966)

### 2. *Kölnische Zeitung*, 28 March 1819: against street lighting
- **Type (de):** "Jede Straßenbeleuchtung ist verwerflich: 1. aus theologischen Gründen, weil sie als Eingriff in die Ordnung Gottes erscheint … die Nacht nicht in Tag verkehren wollen."
- **The seven reasons, in short:**
  1. **Theological:** "Eingriff in die Ordnung Gottes" (interference with God's order).
  2. **Legal:** "Warum soll dieser und jener für eine Einrichtung zahlen, die ihm gleichgültig ist?" (Why should anyone pay for something they don't care about?)
  3. **Medical:** "Die Oel- und Gasausdünstung wirkt nachteilig auf die Gesundheit schwachleibiger und zartnerviger Personen … Schnupfen, Husten und Erkältung" (Oil and gas fumes harm the frail and the nervous… sniffles, coughs and colds).
  4. **Moral:** "die Sittlichkeit wird durch Gassenbeleuchtung verschlimmert … sie verkuppelt verliebte Paare" (street lighting makes morals worse… it pairs off lovers).
  5. **Police:** "sie macht Pferde scheu und Diebe kühn" (it makes horses shy and thieves bold).
  6. **Economic:** the money for fuel "geht jährlich … ins Ausland" (goes abroad every year).
  7. **National:** a nightly "Quasi-Illumination" dulls the festivals.
- **Numbers:** 7 reasons. Six years later, in 1825, Berlin signed a 21-year gas-lighting contract starting with Unter den Linden. The gas works ran from autumn 1826.
- **How it ended:** Dingler (1914): "Dieser Mahnruf verhallte ungehört" ("This warning went unheard").
- **Status:** verified as a published text through two secondary sources: *Journal für Gasbeleuchtung* 1906, p. 879, reprinted verbatim in *Dinglers Polytechnisches Journal* vol. 329 (1914), p. 226. I could not open the 1819 issue itself, because the zeitpunkt.nrw scan is behind a bot check. It is a newspaper opinion piece, not a ruling.
- **Sources:**
  - https://dingler.bbaw.de/articles/ar329054.html
  - https://stadtarchiv.heilbronn.de/stadtgeschichte/unterrichtsmaterial/neuzeit/stromenergie/materialien.html

### 3. Francis I bans printing, Paris, 13 January 1535
- **Context:** the ban followed the Affair of the Placards (Oct 1534).
- **Type (fr):** "abolition de l'imprimerie, et défense d'imprimer des livres dans tout le royaume, sous peine de la hart".
- **Type (en):** "printing abolished, and the printing of books forbidden throughout the kingdom, on pain of the noose".
- **Numbers:** It ran from 13 Jan to 26 Feb 1535, which is 44 days. It was replaced by a plan in which the Parlement named 24 candidates and the king picked 12 censors.
- **How it ended:** "Cette mesure était trop absurde pour ne pas subir des modifications presque immédiates" ("The measure was too absurd not to be changed almost at once"; France Pittoresque).
- **Status:** verified: the letters patent and the lifting are widely documented.
- **Sources:**
  - https://www.france-pittoresque.com/spip.php?article183
  - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Affair_of_the_Placards

### 4. Charles II suppresses the coffee houses, London, 29 December 1675
- **Type:** coffee houses are "the great resort of Idle and disaffected persons", spreading "false, malitious and scandalous reports". They are to be "(for the future) Put down, and suppressed".
- **Numbers:**
  - The ban was due to start on 10 Jan 1676.
  - It was withdrawn by an "Additional Proclamation" on 8 Jan 1676, 10 days after publication and 2 days before it took effect.
  - Coffee sellers were allowed to stay open until 24 Jun 1676, and then nothing more happened.
  - All dates are Old Style.
- **Status:** verified. The text is quoted in Ukers (1922). Both proclamations are catalogued as ESTC R214880 and R226464.
- **Sources:**
  - https://d1lexza0zk46za.cloudfront.net/coursepacks/history/worlds5/docs/coffeehouses.pdf
  - https://www.taylorfrancis.com/chapters/mono/10.4324/9781315094618-7/additional-proclamation-concerning-coffee-houses-london-john-bill-christopher-barker-1675-6-dated-8-january-1675-6-bl-21-2-122-estcr226464-h%C3%BCnersdorff-markman-ellis *(listing only)*

### 5. Frederick the Great against coffee, Prussia, 1777–1787
- **Type:** "My people must drink beer." It comes from the manifesto of 13 Sep 1777, as given in English by Ukers (1922).
- **Type:** *Kaffeeriecher* ("coffee sniffers").
- **Numbers:**
  - A royal roasting monopoly was declared on 21 Jan 1781 ("Déclaration du Roi concernant la vente du café brûlé").
  - About 400 sniffers, mostly war invalids, received a quarter of each fine.
  - They operated from 1781 to 1787.
- **How it ended:** the monopoly was abolished in 1787, after Frederick died in 1786.
- **Status:** the monopoly and the sniffers are verified. The beer quote is **disputed**: it exists only in Ukers' English, and I did not find the German original.
- **Sources:**
  - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coffee_sniffers
  - https://ukersallaboutcoffee.wordpress.com/chapter8/

### 6. Sweden bans coffee five times, 1756–1823
- **Type:** "BANNED 5 TIMES".
- **Numbers:** the bans ran 1756–61, 1766–69, 1794–96, 1799–1802 and 1817–23, under three reigns.
- **How it ended:** the last ban was lifted in 1823. Sweden has since been among the world's highest coffee drinkers per head.
- **Status:** the bans are verified (Knutsson & Hodacs 2021, *Scandinavian Economic History Review*). Gustav III's twin experiment, with one twin on coffee and one on tea, is **apocryphal/unverified**.
- **Source:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gustav_III_of_Sweden%27s_coffee_experiment

### 7. The ribbon loom (*Bandmühle*) bans, 1579–1765
- **Type:** "By an imperial Edict of 19th Feb., 1685, its use was forbidden throughout all Germany. In Hamburg it was burnt in public by order of the Senate."
- **Numbers:**
  - Leiden banned it after weavers' riots; the machine had been in use there since 1629.
  - The Dutch issued decrees in 1632 and 1639, then allowed the loom with conditions on 15 Dec 1661.
  - Cologne banned it in 1676.
  - Emperor Charles VI renewed the edict on 9 Feb 1719.
  - Saxony openly allowed it only in 1765.
  - The loom wove 4–6 pieces at once, and 40–50 in its later form.
- **Legend:** Abbé Lancellotti wrote in 1579 (printed in Venice in 1636) that Danzig's mayor "caused the inventor to be secretly strangled or drowned".
- **How it ended:** the loom won. Marx calls it "the precursor of the mule and the power-loom".
- **Status:** the bans are verified, via Marx, *Capital* I, ch. 15 §5, which draws on Beckmann. The Danzig killing is **apocryphal**: one hearsay source, written 50 years after the fact.
- **Source:** http://la.utexas.edu/users/hcleaver/368/368marxch15sec5table.pdf

### 8. France's calico ban, 1686–1759
- **Type:** the edict of 26 Oct 1686 banned both importing and printing *toiles peintes* (printed calicoes).
- **Numbers:**
  - The ban lasted 73 years, through "more than eighty rulings".
  - Armed smuggling gangs faced death under the 1729 declaration. Women were "whipped, branded and banished".
  - In the Vivarais, 77 men were hanged and 631 sent to the galleys between 1730 and 1780, for all kinds of smuggling, not only calico.
- **How it ended:** an Act of 5 Sep 1759 lifted the ban. From 1760, Oberkampf's factory at Jouy-en-Josas made "Toile de Jouy" world-famous.
- **The 16,000 claim:** Heckscher (*Mercantilism*, 1931, vol. 1 p. 173) wrote that the measures "cost the lives of some 16,000 people, partly through executions and partly through armed affrays". This is **disputed**. It is an old estimate, not a documented count, and the modern thesis below does not repeat it.
- **Sources:**
  - https://platform.ict-tex.eu/pluginfile.php/3706/mod_folder/content/0/Calicoes%20and%20Prohibition%20in%20France.pdf?forcedownload=1 (Crosby, PhD thesis, 2015)
  - https://reason.com/2018/06/25/before-drug-prohibition-there/
  - https://mccloskey.substack.com/p/liberal-ideas-caused-the-innovation *(listing only; quotes Heckscher)*

### 9. England's Calico Acts, 1700 and 1721, repealed 1774
- **Type (1721 title):** "Prohibiting the Use and Wear of all Printed, Painted, Stained or Dyed Callicoes in Apparel, Household Stuff, Furniture, or otherwise".
- **Numbers:**
  - The ban on wearing them applied from 25 Dec 1722.
  - Raw cotton imports grew from 2,000 bales a year to 7,000 by the early 1770s.
  - After the 1774 repeal, demand for raw cotton doubled within a few years, then doubled every decade until the 1840s.
- **Status:** verified. The link between repeal and the boom is Wikipedia's claim, citing Gupta.
- **Source:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calico_Acts

### 10. The Gig Mills Act 1551, repealed 1809
- **Type:** "An Act for the putting down of Gigg-Mills" (5 & 6 Edw. 6 c. 22). Gig mills were machines for finishing woollen cloth.
- **Numbers:** royal assent on 15 Apr 1552. The Woollen Manufacture Act 1809 (royal assent 15 Jun 1809) repealed it in a sweep of old woollen laws; section 1 lists 32 enactments. That makes about 257 years on the books.
- **Status:** verified.
- **Sources:**
  - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_acts_of_the_Parliament_of_England_from_1551
  - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Woollen_Manufacture_Act_1809

### 11. Vespasian refuses a labour-saving machine, Rome, c. AD 69–79
- **Type (la):** "sineret se plebiculam pascere".
- **Type (en):** "You must let me feed my poor commons."
- **Story:** an engineer offered to haul heavy columns up to the Capitol "at small expense". Vespasian gave him "no mean reward" and "refused to make use of it".
- **Status:** verified as ancient text (Suetonius, *Vespasian* 18, c. AD 121). Whether it really happened cannot be known.
- **Source:** https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Suetonius/12Caesars/Vespasian*.html

### 12. Florence's money-changers and Arabic numerals, 1299
- **Type:** amounts must be written "openly and in full by way of letters", not "in the letter of the abacus".
- **Numbers:** the rule dates from 1299. A related Padua rule of 1348 set booksellers' prices "non per cifras, sed per literas claras" ("not in figures but in clear letters").
- **Status:** **disputed framing.** It is a real rule of the Arte del Cambio, the money-changers' guild, but its purpose was to stop fraud, because digits are easy to alter. The popular story that "Florence banned zero" is a modern overreading.
- **Sources:**
  - https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid:71f4c8ca-2b6a-4393-94dc-8d81096985bc/files/m6bbbf02856bf54fe4b723cdb143485d1 ("Medieval Europe's Satanic Ciphers")
  - https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w11022/revisions/w11022.rev0.pdf (Comin, a list of guild bans that also includes Cologne 1397 and Nuremberg 1561)

### 13. *The Quarterly Review*, March 1825, against fast locomotives
- **Type (real wording):**
  - "What, for instance, can be more palpably absurd and ridiculous…"
  - "we should as soon expect the people of Woolwich to suffer themselves to be fired off upon one of Congreve's ricochet rockets, as trust themselves to the mercy of such a machine, going at such a rate".
- **Numbers:**
  - It mocked travel "at the rate of eighteen or twenty miles an hour".
  - It is often quoted as asking Parliament to "limit the speed to eight or nine miles an hour"; I did not check that line against the scan.
  - Vol. 31, no. 62, pp. 361–362.
- **Status:** verified, with one nuance: the familiar one-liner about locomotives "twice as fast as stage-coaches" is a later condensation, made by 1847–1857.
- **Source:** https://quoteinvestigator.com/2019/08/26/locomotive/

### 14. William Preece on the telephone, House of Commons, 2 May 1879
- **Type:** "Here we have a superabundance of messengers, errand boys, and things of that kind. In America they are wanted."
- **Context:** evidence to the Select Committee on Lighting by Electricity (Report, 1879, p. 69). Preece was the Post Office electrician.
- **Status:** verified. The snappier "The Americans have need of the telephone, but we do not" is a **paraphrase**. His other line, "the subdivision of the light is an absolute ignis fatuus" (a will-o'-the-wisp; Feb 1879 lecture), was seen in listings only.
- **Source:** https://geekin9f.wordpress.com/2017/02/15/william-preece-on-messenger-boys-vs-telephones-worst-technological-prediction-of-all-time/

### 15. UK Electric Lighting Act 1882 and its buy-out clock
- **Type:** section 27 let local authorities buy out private electricity companies after **21 years**.
- **Numbers:**
  - The first draft said 15 years; the House of Lords made it 21.
  - The Electric Lighting Act 1888 (28 Jun 1888) raised it to **42 years**, and valued a company as a going concern rather than at scrap value.
  - In 1878 Britain had just 20 electric lighting installations.
- **Status:** the law is verified. Whether it acted as a brake is **disputed**. Many said it "stifled private enterprise", but the historian Leslie Hannah argues that 21 years was "an eternity to most investors".
- **Source:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_Lighting_Acts_1882_to_1909

### 16. France's telegraph monopoly, 2 May 1837, and the first network hack
- **Type (fr):** "Quiconque transmettra, sans autorisation, des signaux d'un lieu à un autre, soit à l'aide de machines télégraphiques, soit par tout autre moyen, sera puni d'un emprisonnement d'un mois à un an et d'une amende de 1 000 à 10 000 francs."
- **Type (en):** "Whoever transmits signals from one place to another without authorisation, by telegraph machines or by any other means, shall be punished with one month to one year in prison and a fine of 1,000 to 10,000 francs."
- **Backstory:**
  - From 1834 to 1836, the Blanc twins of Bordeaux bribed operators on the state's Chappe optical telegraph at Tours. The operators slipped stock-market moves into state messages.
  - A parcel of socks meant the market was down; gloves meant it was up.
  - The twins were acquitted on 14 Mar 1837 because no law covered what they had done.
- **How it ended:** the state telecom monopoly lasted until EU-wide liberalisation of voice telephony on 1 Jan 1998 (Directive 96/19/EC).
- **Status:** the law and the hack are verified. The "1837 → 1998" arc is my narrative link: the monopoly continued under the PTT, the French state post and telecom service.
- **Sources:**
  - https://www.persee.fr/doc/comm_0588-8018_1974_num_21_1_1312
  - https://france-pittoresque.com/spip.php?article15342
  - https://fr.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piratage_du_t%C3%A9l%C3%A9graphe_Chappe
  - https://www.senat.fr/rap/r01-273/r01-27328.html *(listing only)*

### 17. Jonas Hanway and the umbrella, London, 1750s–1786
- **Type:** "in spite of the jeers of the passengers and the clamour of the chairmen and hackney coachmen, he saw his own practice generally adopted".
- **Numbers:** he persevered "for some thirty years" and died in 1786.
- **Status:** verified as the *Dictionary of National Biography*'s account, which hedges: he "is said to have been the first man…". Stories of him being pelted with rubbish are folklore.
- **Source:** https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Dictionary_of_National_Biography,_1885-1900/Hanway,_Jonas

### 18. The fork scandal, Venice, 1004
- **Story:** the Byzantine noblewoman Maria Argyropoulina married Giovanni Orseolo, son of the Doge of Venice, in 1004. She died of plague in 1006 or 1007. About 50 years later, Peter Damian attacked her fork, her perfumes and her bathing in dew.
- **Status:** the criticism is verified. The line "God in his wisdom has provided man with natural forks—his fingers" is **apocryphal/unsourced**.
- **Source:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maria_Argyropoulina

## Part 2. Myths: handle as LEGEND or debunk on screen

### 19. Elizabeth I refuses William Lee's stocking-frame patent (1589)
- **Type (attributed):** "Thou aimest high, Master Lee. Consider thou what the invention could do to my poor subjects. It would assuredly bring to them ruin by depriving them of employment, thus making them beggars."
- **Facts:**
  - Lee's frame was invented around 1589; he later raised it from 8 to 20 needles per inch.
  - He left for Rouen with his brother, 9 workers and 9 frames, under a patent from Henry IV.
  - The last record of him dates from 1614.
- **Status:** **apocryphal.** No contemporary source records an audience with the Queen. The story first appears in Gravenor Henson's *History of the Framework Knitters* (1831), from knitters' oral tradition.
- **Sources:**
  - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Lee_(inventor)
  - https://conversableeconomist.com/2019/03/13/the-story-of-william-lee-and-his-knitting-machine/ *(listing only)*

### 20. Bavarian doctors warn of "delirium furiosum" (1835)
- **Claim:** the Royal Bavarian medical college warned that trains would give riders "a special kind of delirium furiosum" and wanted the tracks fenced so that onlookers would be spared.
- **Status:** **apocryphal.**
  - Railway historian Wolfgang Mück found no such medical college in Bavaria around 1835.
  - *Die Zeit* (2005, no. 37) calls it "frei erfunden" ("freely invented").
  - The sociologist Bernward Joerges traced the story in "Expertise Lost" (*Social Studies of Science* 24:1, 1994).
- **Counter-fact for type:** the Nuremberg–Fürth line opened on 7 Dec 1835, 6 km long, pulled by the locomotive *Adler*. It carried 475,219 passengers in its first year, with trains pulled alternately by horses and steam.
- **Sources:**
  - https://www.ice-treff.de/index.php?id=345546 (links the Zeit, FAZ and IZGMF pieces)
  - https://doi.org/10.1177/030631279402400105 *(listing only)*
  - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bavarian_Ludwig_Railway
  - https://www.dhm.de/lemo/rueckblick/lemo-rueckblick-erste-eisenbahnen-in-deutschland (475,219 passengers)

### 21. Kaiser Wilhelm II: "I believe in the horse"
- **Type (attributed):** "Ich glaube an das Pferd. Das Automobil ist nur eine vorübergehende Erscheinung." ("I believe in the horse. The automobile is only a passing phenomenon.")
- **Status:** **apocryphal.** It first appears around 2000 on Usenet, then in a 2001 book and *Die Welt* in 2005. It is not in any digitised text from before the 21st century.
- **Real quote:** Wilhelm did say "vorübergehende Erscheinung" on 10 Jan 1900, but about Social Democracy. He also owned cars.
- **Source:** https://falschzitate.blogspot.com/2018/01/das-auto-ist-eine-vorubergehende.html

### 22. Parmentier and the potato (France, 1748–1787)
- **Claims:**
  - A parlement banned growing potatoes in 1748 for causing leprosy.
  - Parmentier guarded his field by day only, so that people would steal the crop at night.
- **Facts:** the Paris Faculty of Medicine declared potatoes edible in 1772.
- **Status:**
  - The 1748 ban is **disputed**. It is widely repeated, but sources disagree on whether the Parlement of Paris or of Besançon issued it, and I found no primary text.
  - The guarded field is **apocryphal**. Julien-Joseph Virey attributed it to Parmentier after his death; Wikipedia calls it "almost certainly not true".
- **Sources:**
  - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antoine-Augustin_Parmentier
  - https://revue-histoire.fr/histoire-moderne/la-pomme-de-terre-star-de-nos-assiettes/

### 23. Other myths, in brief
- **Ottoman printing ban (1485 Bayezid II, 1515 Selim I)** — **disputed.**
  - The ban rests on André Thevet, an unreliable writer. No Ottoman decree has been found.
  - Presses of non-Muslim communities ran in Istanbul from 1493 or 1504.
  - Müteferrika won permission in 1727 and printed his first book in 1729. By 1743 he had printed 17 works in 23 volumes.
  - Sources: https://www.ageofinvention.xyz/p/age-of-invention-did-the-ottomans · https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ibrahim_Muteferrika
- **Gregory XVI: "chemins de fer, chemins d'enfer"** ("iron roads, roads to hell") — **apocryphal.** Wikipedia: "there is no document or public statement from the Pope banning or saying anything of the sort". Railway projects in the Papal States were postponed until 1846.
  - Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pope_Gregory_XVI
- **Tiberius's flexible glass** — **apocryphal**, and Pliny himself doubted it. The workshop was "completely destroyed for fear that the value of metals such as copper, silver and gold would otherwise be lowered". Pliny adds that the story was "current through frequent repetition rather than" authentic (*Natural History* 36.195).
  - Source: https://www.attalus.org/pliny/hn36b.html
- **Friedrich Wilhelm III on the Berlin–Potsdam railway (opened 29 Oct 1838)** — **disputed.**
  - The attributed line is "Kann mir keine große Glückseligkeit davon versprechen, ein paar Stunden früher von Berlin in Potsdam zu sein." ("I can't expect much bliss from reaching Potsdam a few hours sooner.")
  - I found only a paraphrase in local history and no primary source.
  - Source: https://www.janecke.name/ortsgeschichte/preussische-stammbahn-1838/

## Part 3. Europe ships (positive counter-stories)

### 24. Gutenberg's 42-line Bible, Mainz, c. 1454–55
- **Type:** "extremely neat and legible … [you] would be able to read it without effort, and indeed without glasses". This is the future Pope Pius II, writing to Cardinal Carvajal in March 1455, often dated 12 Mar.
- **Numbers:** 158 or 180 copies were printed. 49 survive at least in substantial part, 21 of them complete.
- **Status:** verified.
- **Sources:**
  - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gutenberg_Bible
  - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johannes_Gutenberg

### 25. Venice's patent statute, 19 March 1474
- **Type:** "men … capable of devising and inventing all manner of ingenious contrivances". It protects "any new ingenious contrivance, not made heretofore in our dominion".
- **Numbers:** protection for up to 10 years; infringers had to pay 100 ducats and the copy was "immediately destroyed". It is Europe's first statutory patent system.
- **Status:** verified.
- **Source:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Venetian_Patent_Statute

### 26. Bertha Benz's drive, 5 August 1888 (and patent DRP 37435, 1886)
- **Type:** she drove "without telling her husband and without permission of the authorities".
- **Numbers:**
  - Karl Benz's patent was German patent no. 37435, granted in 1886.
  - Bertha drove about 106 km, from Mannheim to Pforzheim.
  - She was 39; her sons were 13 and 15.
- **Status:** verified.
- **Sources:**
  - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bertha_Benz
  - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benz_Patent-Motorwagen

### 27. Airbus A300, Ariane 1, Schengen, Erasmus

### 28. GSM: Copenhagen, 7 September 1987 to Helsinki, 1 July 1991
- **Type:** the Memorandum of Understanding (MoU), signed in Copenhagen by **15 operators from 13 countries** on 7 Sep 1987. It set a common date to launch digital mobile networks across Europe.
- **The first call, 1 Jul 1991:**
  - Former prime minister Harri Holkeri, in Helsinki, called Kaarina Suonio, deputy mayor of Tampere.
  - It ran on Radiolinja's network with Nokia gear, on 900 MHz. It reportedly lasted just over 3 minutes *(listing only)*.
- **Status:** verified. Note that a Nokia engineer, Pekka Lonka, made a test call a few hours earlier, and Radiolinja made a test call on 27 Mar 1991.
- **Sources:**
  - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GSM
  - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harri_Holkeri
  - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radiolinja
  - https://www.gsma.com/newsroom/press-release/global-mobile-communication-is-20-years-old/ *(listing only)*
