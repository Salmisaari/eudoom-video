# euroboom: public-domain archive bank

Built 2026-09-29 for the euroboom music video (Europe and AI acceleration, tech-Twitter audience). Each image is meant to be used "sideways": it rhymes with a lyric and carries an insider meaning.

**97 items: 93 OK worldwide, 0 OK non-commercial only, 4 not OK.** The not-OK items are listed at the end so nobody reaches for them by accident.

- The same data, machine-readable, is in `archive.json`.
- Previews are in `archive_thumbs/<id>.jpg`. They are at most 800 px and under 300 KB, and are for looking only; use the hi-res links for production.

## How to read this

- **Rights as shown** is the statement copied from the page where the rights were checked. That is either:
  - the museum's own item page,
  - the Wikimedia Commons file page (licence template and source), or
  - the Internet Archive item page.

  When an image is hosted on Commons, the holding institution is named. If the institution's own page could be reached, it was checked as well; see the item notes.
- **Conclusion** takes one of three values:
  - `OK worldwide`: the underlying work is public domain in both the EU and the US, and the reproduction is CC0/PDM or a faithful 2D copy. For the EU that means the maker died in 1955 or earlier, or the work is anonymous and was published more than 70 years ago. For the US it means published before 1931. On reproductions, the EU DSM Directive Art. 14 says a faithful reproduction of a public-domain artwork gets no new rights; in the US, Bridgeman v. Corel says the same.
  - `OK non-commercial only`: only available from a host with Gallica-type terms.
  - `not OK`: protected in the EU or US, or only available under CC BY/BY-SA.
- **Themes**: the first tag decides which section an item sits in. The main keys are fake-intelligence, proto-computing, speed, bubbles, bureaucracy, doom, idleness, museum, hallucination and wildcard. Extra tags: alignment, scaling, compute, energy, regulation, self-image, music, labour.
- **Films**: the link is the film file. The preview is a poster frame. Strip any soundtrack; see the item notes.
- These conclusions are a careful research call, not legal advice. Anything with a caveat says so in the item notes.

## The 15 strongest

1. **The Turk, Tab. III: cutaway showing the hidden operator** (`racknitz-turk-hidden-operator`, Joseph Friedrich zu Racknitz, 1789): The Mechanical Turk itself, in the one plate that shows the man inside. Demo vs. production, Builder.ai and human-in-the-loop 'AI' in a single hand-coloured, high-res image; pairs with the doors-open demo plate.
2. **Interior of Vaucanson's Automatic Duck** (`vaucanson-digesting-duck-diagram`, Unknown illustrator, 21 January 1899): A confident engineering cutaway of a mechanism nobody had seen, for a machine whose headline feature was faked. Clean line art that survives any halftone.
3. **The Rhinoceros** (`durer-rhinoceros-1515`, Albrecht Dürer, 1515): The canonical text-to-image hallucination: drawn from a letter, wrong in confident detail, copied for 250 years. Met CC0 scan of the woodcut.
4. **The same city woodcut used for Damascus, Mantua, Ferrara and Macedonia** (`nuremberg-chronicle-same-woodcut`, Michael Wolgemut and Wilhelm Pleydenwurff workshop, 1493): Four cities, one woodblock: mode collapse explained with no caption. Works as a 4-up split screen.
5. **Landscape with the Fall of Icarus** (`bruegel-fall-of-icarus`, After Pieter Bruegel the Elder, c. 1560s): Hangs in Brussels. The ploughman keeps working while Icarus splashes in the corner: the video's whole thesis in one frame, with Auden's poem as a free subtext.
6. **Démolition d'un mur** (`lumiere-demolition-mur-reversible`, Louis Lumière, 1896): A wall that reassembles itself from dust when run backwards reads as diffusion denoising with no explanation, and it is motion footage, not a still.
7. **Accident de train de la gare Montparnasse du 22 octobre 1895** (`montparnasse-derailment-1895`, Hippolyte Blancard, 1895): The most graphic image of acceleration overshooting its terminus (a locomotive nose-down through the station wall). Paris Musées CC0 photo.
8. **Frontispiece to Thomas Hobbes, 'Leviathan, or the Matter, Forme, & Power of a Common-wealth'** (`hobbes-leviathan-frontispiece`, Abraham Bosse, 1651): A sovereign literally made of thousands of people: the European regulator as a model trained on everyone. British Library CC0.
9. **Isambard Kingdom Brunel Standing Before the Launching Chains of the Great Eastern** (`howlett-brunel-launching-chains`, Robert Howlett, 1857): The scaling-pilled founder posed against the capex. Iconic, CC0, and it pairs with Daumier's cartoon of the same ship failing to launch.
10. **Why the Tower of Babel could not reach the Moon** (`kircher-tower-to-the-moon`, Athanasius Kircher, 1679): A 1679 diagram 'proving' that scaling can't reach the Moon: scaling-law skepticism as a single vertical line, which is also a great frame.
11. **Der Zauberlehrling** (`barth-sorcerers-apprentice-1882`, Ferdinand Barth, 1882): The alignment parable, with the line every alignment talk quotes ('Die ich rief, die Geister...'). A dramatic, high-contrast engraving.
12. **Joseph Marie Jacquard** (`jacquard-woven-silk-portrait`, Didier, Petit et Cie, 1839): A photorealistic image generated from about 24,000 punched cards in 1839. The literal ancestor of image models, made in Lyon.
13. **Le Répertoire Bibliographique Universel** (`otlet-repertoire-bibliographique-1900`, Paul Otlet / Office International de Bibliographie, c. 1900): Brussels ran a search engine on index cards in 1900: clerks at endless card cabinets. 'Europe built Google and filed it' reads instantly.
14. **Europa Regina** (`munster-europa-regina`, Sebastian Münster, 1598 edition): Europe's self-portrait as a crowned queen, with Britain already floating off. Pure continental self-regard, and a big, graphic map.
15. **Le Mélomane** (`melies-melomane`, Georges Méliès, 1903): Made for a music video: the bandmaster multiplies his own head into notes on a telegraph-wire staff. Generative music out of copies of yourself, as a moving image.

## Flags, gaps and source log

### Gallica / BnF
- Nothing in the bank is Gallica-only.
- Gallica stopped answering from this machine during the session (timeouts, then connection resets). Because of that, Gallica-only candidates were left out rather than added unverified:
  - Marinetti's Futurist manifesto on Le Figaro's front page (20 Feb 1909). The only free copy on Commons is a CC BY 3.0 photo of the page. A Gallica scan would be non-commercial only.
  - Higher-res scans of Jules Beau's 1899 Jamais Contente photos.
- The Commons copy of Gravelot's printed Vaucanson frontispiece is a Gallica scan. The bank uses the Carnavalet drawing instead (Paris Musées, CC0).

### Library of Congress
Every automated request was stopped by a Cloudflare challenge (curl, WebFetch and a real headless Chrome). No LoC items were added, and nothing here depends on an LoC page that wasn't read.

### Other blocked or awkward sites
- **NGA Washington**: item pages return 403. NGA material appears only via NGA's own CC0 uploads to Commons (the Icarus engraving is referenced).
- **The Met**: plain requests get HTTP 429. Every Met item was verified by rendering the page in a headless browser, where it shows the "Public Domain" badge, and by the API field `isPublicDomain: true`. The site footer's "All rights reserved" refers to the website, not the Open Access images.
- **Atget at the Met**: Atget photos there are flagged `isPublicDomain: false` (many are later prints), so none were used.
- **Humboldt University digi-hub** (Racknitz Turk plates): sits behind an anti-bot proof-of-work wall. The rights were read on the Commons file pages instead.
- **Frans Hals Museum, KHM, Louvre, Prado, National Gallery London, Musée d'Orsay, RMFAB Brussels**: their own object pages were not located or not checked. Those paintings are used via Commons PD-Art copies, which is noted per item. The museums' own download terms don't bind Commons copies, and under DSM Art. 14 faithful reproductions of public-domain paintings carry no new rights.

### Sources and what they yielded
- **Rijksmuseum**: 11 items, all "Copyright: Public domain" (PDM) on the item page.
- **The Met**: 18 items, all Open Access / CC0.
- **Paris Musées / Carnavalet**: 4 items, CC0.
- **Art Institute of Chicago, SMB Berlin, SMK Copenhagen, Tekniska museet / DigitaltMuseum, Finnish National Gallery (via Finna, CC0)**: 1 item each.
- **Internet Archive**: 6 book or journal scans.
- **Wikimedia Commons**: the remaining 49, each with a licence template and a named source.
- **Europeana**: used for discovery with the PD/CC0 filter only. Its broader "open" filter also returns CC BY-SA scans (for example Deutsche Fotothek's Turk plates), and those were rejected.
- **Nationalmuseum Sweden**: probed, but no item made the cut.
- **Public Domain Review**: not used directly, because the web-search quota ran out. Its greatest hits (Kircher, Seba, the vegetable lamb, the Turk) are covered from primary sources.

### Film rules applied
- The EU film term runs 70 years after the last death among director, screenwriter, dialogue author and composer. The US rule is publication before 1931.
- Lumière films are PD in the EU only since 1 Jan 2025 (Auguste Lumière d. 1954).
- Several HD files come from 2016 Institut Lumière or DVD restorations. Commons treats these as PD; older transfers are linked where they exist.
- Strip every soundtrack. One YouTube-sourced Lumière upload is explicitly tagged `{{Non-free audio}}`.

- **Jaquet-Droz automata**: only modern CC BY-SA 2.0 FR photos turned up, and no verifiable public-domain engraving.
- **A Jacquard loom engraving**: the free scans found were Wellcome, CC BY 4.0. The woven Jacquard portrait covers the idea.
- **Holbein's Astrologer**: not surfaced by the Met or Rijksmuseum search APIs. Conrad Meyer's 1650 'Astrologus' stands in.
- **A "closed for holidays" image and Grand Tour engravings**: nothing strong enough with clean rights.
